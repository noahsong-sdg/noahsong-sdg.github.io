Some quotations and thoughts I had in reading Carnegie's autobio! Largely unorganized, but one main theme is how his views on fundamental human dignity inform his actions and beliefs about himself:

> As I look back upon the early struggles this can be said: there was not a prouder family in the land. A keen sense of honor, independence, self-respect, pervaded the household. (Ch3)

Said to President Harrison in argument that the US should not go to war with Chile:
> Mr. President, no man can dishonor me except myself. Honor wounds must be self-inflicted. (Ch27)

Those beliefs are seen through his actions towards others. After recounting a story of how a blacksmith's intervention averted certain danger to the company:
> I have had many incidents, such as that of the blacksmith, in my life. Slight attentions or a kind word to the humble often bring back reward as great as it is unlooked for. No kind action is ever lost. Even to this day I occasionally meet men whom i had forgotten, who recall some trifling attention I have been able to pay them, especially when in charge at Washington of government railways and telegraphs during the Civil War, when I could pass people within the lines -- a father helped to reach a wounded or sick son at the front, or enabled to bring home his remains, or some similar service. I am indebted to these trifles for some of the happiest attentions and the most pleasing incidents of my life. (Ch7)

Ie: luck surface area expands with compassionate action! Or, not seeing life as zero sum.
>I remember being deeply affected by the reception I met with when I returned to my division... I knew how much I cared for them and it was pleasing to know that they reciprocated my feelings. Working-men always do reciprocate kindly feeling. If we truly care for others we need not be anxious about their feelings for us. Like draws to like. (Ch8)

> A great business is seldom if ever built up, except on lines of the strictest integrity. A reputation for "cuteness" and sharp dealing is fatal in great affairs. Not the letter of the law, but the spirit, must be the rule. (Ch12)

These beliefs can be summed up in what he says about Americans. In taking two Pittsburgh workers around New York and lunches with them as social equals:
> There is one great difference between the American working-man and the foreigner. The American is a man; he sits down at lunch with people as if he were -- as he generally is -- a gentleman born. It is splendid. (Ch28)

In these quotations lies a good lesson: to treat others, no matter their station, as social equals. For some interactions, this means to aid and to be generous when you are able regardless of utilitarian gain. For others, to remember your own dignity and honor and to forget sycophancy and people-pleasing.

Carnegie quotes Robert Burns in summary:
> The rank is but the guinea's stamp,
> The Man's the gowd for a' that.

Another major theme is found in his quotation of a Scottish proverb: "The gods send thread for a web begun" (Ch19)

> My advice to young men would be not only to concentrate their whole time and attention on the wone business in life in which they engage, but to put every dollar of their capital into it. If there be any business that willl not bear extension, the true policy is to invest the surplus in first-class securities which will yield a moderate but certain revenue if some other growing business cannot be found. As for myself my decision was taken early. I would concentrate upon the manufacture of iron and steel and be master in that.

> A sunny disposition is worth more than fortune. Young people should know that it can be cultivated; that the mind like the body can be moved from the shade into sunshine. Let us move it then. Laugh trouble away if possible, and one usually can if he be anything of a philosopher, provided that self-reproach comes not from his own wrongdoing. (Ch1)

> This policy is the true secret of success. Uphill work it will be for a few years until your work is proven, but after that it is smooth sailing. (Ch9)

And some miscellaneous quotations I thought were fun:

He began "ceasing to struggle for more wealth" once profits reached "forty millions  of dollars per year and the prospect of increased earnings before us was amazing". This is around 1.61B today in terms of purchasing power. In terms of percentage of GDP, $40 million represented about 0.19% of the 1900 U.S. economy; the same share today is ~ $61 billion.

Carnegie and his peers started the "Webster Literary Society", a small debating club, wherein they discussed policy and theology:
> We young men were getting to be pretty wild boys about theology, although more truly reverent about religion. (Ch6)

Carnegie &c sabbathmaxxed:
> It was the custom of our party to spend Sunday afternoons in the woods. (Ch6)

When presented with the opportunity to buy ten shares of Adams Express stock, Carnegie's mother pledged their house as security for a loan to buy this stock. What a gangster!

General Grant was very ordinary-looking: a big L for physiognomy?
> I remember that Secretary of War Stanton said that when he visited the armies in the West, General Grant and his staff entered his car; he looked at them, one after the other, as they entered and seeing General Grant, said to himself, "Well, I do not know which is General Grant, but there is one that cannot be." Yet this was he. (Ch8)

> The world traveler who gives careful study to teh bibles of the various religions of the East will be well repaid. (Ch 14)

Interestingly, he states that his past success was a point against him in his advances toward Miss Whitfield:
> My wealth and future plans were against me. I was rich and had everything and she felt she could be of little use or benefit to me. Her ideal was to be the real helpmeet of a young, struggling man to whom she could and would be indispensable, as her mother had been to her father. (Ch15)
