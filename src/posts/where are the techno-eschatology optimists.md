American evangelicalism is monolithically [premillennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premillennialism), precipitated by John Darby's dispensationalism during the Third Great Awakening.  Its okay if these terms are unfamiliar to you; the thing to understand about this belief-set is 1) a general eschatological pessimism and 2) an *adventus*, and not *futurum*, view of the eschaton. I think these two features inhere in Darbyism/dispensationalism, but won't argue that here. At least, I don't think its disagreeable to say these dispositions correlate.

For example, culture imbues the belief that the eschaton inaugurates in great calamity. When we think about """eschatology""" or """Revelation""", what instinctively comes to mind are verses about the sun disappearing, stars falling from the sky, mountains being moved into the ocean, so on. Its not hard to perceive how these views make it discouraging for Christians to build institutions or to be involved in technology (John Lennox eg draws parallels between agi and the antichrist)

There's a panoply of historical/exegetical commentary against pessimistic eschatologies and quietistic dispositions (one of my favorite essays on this [here](https://www.staseos.net/post/victory-in-eschatological-conflict)) which I think is (re)gaining popularity. Surprisingly, there's a dearth of such thought in relation to technology! [1] Indeed, the writers most famous for techno-eschatological optimism are so niche that name dropping them might farm big brain aura

eg, Nikolai Fedorov (1827-1903) is maybe the origin for transhumanist language in arguing for the resurrection of the dead being achieved by technical means (although Fedorov sees these means as being grounded in a robust Christian faith). Obscure now, he was not when he was alive, and had many admirers (all quotations taken from Burdett ch2):
- Dostoevsky writes "... I must say that in essence I completely agree with his ideas. I read them as if they were my own."
- Tolstoy states "Do not be afraid, I do not share his views, but I do understand them to such an extent that I feel capable of defending them and of giving them preference over any other views."
- Tsiolkovsky, the father of Russian rocketry, was "saved from committing suicide by Fedorov and it was through his mentorship that Tsiolkovsky became an outspoken advocate of exploring space and 'the creator of a Utopian scheme for the reorganization of the universe'".

Or, Pierre Teilhard de Chardin (1881 - 1955), who comes soon after Fedorov and writes on very similar issues. There's much to say about Teilhard, but I'll quote from an [essay](https://www.staseos.net/post/understanding-teilhard-part-i-the-holiness-of-evolution)where I first learned about him:

> Teilhard describes evolution as a “process of increasing complication whereby Matter contrives to arrange itself in particles of ever greater volume, ever more highly organized.”
>
> ...
>
> More importantly, Teilhard is struck by the fact that evolution represents the antithesis of entropy. The evolutionary process shines uniquely against the otherwise-hopeless backdrop of a cosmos darkened by the second law of thermodynamics.
>
> As we look around us, we see that both our bodies and the universe itself tend towards decay and annihilation. Through evolution, however, God has created a process which runs contrary to entropy and prefigures its ultimate defeat. In Teilhard’s words, “something in the cosmos escapes from entropy, and does so more and more.”
>
> Evolution is the germ of the universe’s eventual transformation and renewal in the New Heaven and the New Earth. It is a spark of the conflagration which will consume The Last Enemy that Shall Be Destroyed. “Our world contains within itself a mysterious promise of the future,” implicit in evolution, Teilhard wrote. No sooner had I begun to imagine Teilhard’s vision of evolution as a “germ” than Teilhard used the image himself: “the divine cosmos germinates from the ruins of the old earth.”

I plan on writing more on Teilhard's dialectic later.

If Christianity is to robustly reject quietism in favor of Kuyperian theology, we need to put thought to how work and technology and "co-creation" are placed in our anthropology. Because any real endeavor requires macroscopic vision crafting, this techno-eschatological ideology must precede long lasting Christian institutions. Fortunately, Teilhard has done much of the work for us, and we can pick up where he left off.
